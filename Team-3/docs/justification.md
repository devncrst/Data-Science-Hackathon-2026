# Written justification

**Model design.** Building age has two signals that hold across cities. One is temporal: construction after 1984 leaves a break in the Landsat series, and older fabric weathers slowly. The other is spatial: whole districts are built in one era, and 87% of neighbouring Amsterdam pixels share a class. Stage 1 therefore has two Madrid-trained parts. The first is a logistic regression on the organiser's 60 temporal features, each averaged over the pixel's 90 m neighbourhood. The second is six small 1-D CNNs that read the full 42-year, 12-channel series of each pixel and of its neighbourhood. Three of the CNNs are trained with a domain-adversarial discriminator fed unlabelled Amsterdam pixels, which pushes their features toward properties both cities share. Madrid 5-fold cross-validation gives macro F1 0.715 ± 0.007 (organiser forest 0.628), and zero-shot Amsterdam F1 rises from 0.443 to 0.594.

**Transfer strategy.** With n labelled Amsterdam pixels per class, every Madrid head is re-fitted as a logistic regression whose weights are penalised for moving away from the Madrid weights. Few labels therefore make small corrections, and many labels can override Madrid. The heads are averaged (one third features, two thirds CNNs). Probabilities are then smoothed over the neighbour graph by label propagation, with the labelled pixels clamped, so each label also informs its surrounding block.

**Interpreting the F1 scores.** On the organiser's evaluation protocol, Amsterdam macro F1 is 0.642 (5 labels per class), 0.698 (25), 0.715 (50), 0.736 (100) and 0.748 (200). That averages 0.708, against 0.596 for the organiser baseline, and the pre-registered check seed gives 0.710. Even at 5 labels the gain is +0.10, because the Madrid prior and spatial propagation carry the scarce-data regime. Gains continue rather than plateau. Settings were tuned on the provided Amsterdam data, so hidden-test scores may be slightly lower.
