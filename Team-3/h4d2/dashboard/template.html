Dating a new city's buildings with a handful of labels

Our model, h4d2, looks at 42 years of satellite photos of every small patch of a city and guesses when the buildings there were built. It learned in Madrid, where we had lots of answers, and then needed only 5 to 200 example answers per age group to work in Amsterdam.

Start here: what this is, in plain words

No machine-learning background needed. Read this once and the rest of the page will make sense.

1The question

Cities often don't know when each of their buildings was built. That matters for planning, heritage and energy upgrades. Checking by hand is slow and expensive.

2The data

A satellite (Landsat) has photographed every 30 m × 30 m square of the city once a year since 1984. Each square gets a 42-year "flip-book" in 6 colours, including infrared we can't see.

3The trick

New and old neighbourhoods change differently over the years. The model learns those patterns in Madrid, where many answers are known, then reuses them in Amsterdam.

4The test

In Amsterdam the model gets only a few answers (5 to 200 per age group). It must guess every other square, and we score how many it gets right.

The words you'll see on this page

Pixel / patch

One 30 m × 30 m square of the city, seen from space. Each dot on the maps is one patch.

Age class

The four answer boxes: built before 1945, 1945–1984, 1984–2004, or 2004–2024 (in Madrid the first cut-off is 1960). The model must put every patch in one box.

Model

A computer program that learned a rule from examples instead of being told the rule. Ours is called h4d2.

Training

Showing the model many examples with the right answer, so it can adjust itself until its guesses match.

Label

A patch whose real age we tell the model. Labels are expensive, because someone has to look them up.

Label budget (n)

How many labelled patches per age class the model may use in Amsterdam. n = 5 means 5 × 4 = 20 labels in total.

Few-shot / transfer learning

Learning in one place with lots of examples (Madrid), then adapting to a new place with very few (Amsterdam).

Zero-shot

Using the Madrid model in Amsterdam with no Amsterdam labels at all. It shows how much carries over on its own.

Baseline

The organisers' simple reference model. Beating it by a clear margin is how you show real progress.

Score: macro F1

The main score, from 0 to 1. For each age class it checks how many of those buildings we found and how many of our calls were right, then averages the four classes equally so rare ages count as much as common ones. 1.0 is perfect; random guessing scores about 0.25.

Recall

For one age class: of all patches that really are that age, the share the model got right.

Confusion matrix

A table of real age (rows) against the model's guess (columns). The diagonal is correct; everything else is a mix-up.

Draws and seed

Which patches get labelled is picked at random, so we repeat each test 10 times with different picks (draws) and average. The seed is the number that fixes the random picks, so anyone can repeat them exactly.

Neighbours

Patches within 90 m. Buildings next to each other are usually from the same era, so the model also looks at the neighbourhood.

Hidden test

Data the organisers keep secret and use to score every team fairly. Our numbers here use the data we were given, so hidden-test scores may be a little lower.

Why neighbours matter

87% of side-by-side Amsterdam patches are the same age, because whole districts were built in one go. So the model doesn't judge a patch alone: it also looks at the patches around it, up to 90 m away.

How neighbours changed the guess

The model first guesses each patch on its own. Then it "asks the neighbours": it blends its guess with the guesses for the patches within 90 m. Below is a real 7 × 7 corner of Amsterdam.

Does asking the neighbours help? Score over the 10 official tests

Our official scores include this step. Honest result: blending costs a little when labels are scarce and helps at 200. Most of h4d2's lead comes from starting with what it learned in Madrid and from the neural networks, not from this step.

05

Run your own data

Have a file of predictions? Drop it here to see it on a map. It needs one row per patch, with columns px_key, py_key (the patch's position) and pred_class (the guessed age class, 1 to 4). The files made by scripts/submit_predict.sh already have this. If the file also has the real answers (true_class or weighted_mean_year), you also get a score. To run the full model on raw satellite data, use the model bench, or h4d2/Dashboard.ipynb on the hub.

06

How h4d2 works

Four parts. Each one helps carry what the model learned in Madrid over to Amsterdam.

1 · Neighbour context

From each patch's 42-year flip-book we compute 60 summary numbers, such as how bright it was early on and when it changed most. These are averaged with the patches up to 90 m around it, because neighbours are usually the same age.

2 · Six networks

Neural networks are pattern-finding programs. Six of them read each patch's full 42-year series. Three were also trained so they can't tell Madrid from Amsterdam, which pushes them to learn patterns that hold in both cities (this is called domain-adversarial training).

3 · Gentle adaptation

With the few Amsterdam answers, the model adjusts its final decision rule but is held close to the rule it learned in Madrid. Think of it as "trust Madrid, nudge for Amsterdam", so a handful of examples can't throw it off.

4 · Smoothing

Each final guess is blended with its neighbours' guesses, and the known answers pass their age on to nearby patches. Section 04 shows exactly what this changes on real patches.

Disclosures

What we want judges to know: three networks were trained on Amsterdam satellite data without any age answers. When predicting, Amsterdam patches are also used as neighbours, again without answers. Our settings were chosen using the Amsterdam data we were given, so hidden-test scores may be a little lower. Cheating check: we scrambled the real ages of every patch the model wasn't told, and 0 predictions changed, so the model never peeks at the answers.
