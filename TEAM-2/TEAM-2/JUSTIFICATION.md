# Written justification

**Model design.** Each 30 m building pixel is described by 60 temporal features from 40 years of harmonised Landsat data (notebook 3). We add the mean of its eight neighbouring pixels, following Tobler's first law of geography: neighbourhoods are usually built in the same era. Each city is standardised on its own data, without labels, which removes sensor and climate offsets between cities. A class-balanced LightGBM model (400 trees, seed 42) is trained on Madrid and reaches macro-F1 0.680 in 5-fold cross-validation (baseline 0.618). Among five model families, LightGBM scored best in Madrid; a neural network came close there but transferred worst.

**Transfer strategy.** With n labelled Amsterdam pixels per class, we average two adapters: fine-tuning (50 extra trees grown on the Madrid model with the local labels) and a logistic regression on local features plus Madrid's predicted probabilities. Fine-tuning keeps what Madrid learned and corrects it; the regression stays stable when labels are few. Averaging beat either alone. We rejected ideas that helped Madrid but hurt Amsterdam: 156 combined features scored 0.696 in Madrid but only 0.639 in Amsterdam.

**Interpreting scores.** Over 10 random label draws, our Amsterdam macro-F1 averages 0.669 across n = 5 to 200 (baseline 0.596), and 0.660 ± 0.018 at n = 25 (baseline 0.599). We beat the organisers' prototype few-shot method at every n (average 0.605). Most errors fall between neighbouring age classes. A backup without neighbour features still averages 0.656. Shuffled labels score 0.247, chance level. On unseen 1 km blocks Madrid scores 0.616, which we report beside 0.680; in Amsterdam, removing test pixels next to labelled ones moves n = 25 only from 0.660 to 0.658.
